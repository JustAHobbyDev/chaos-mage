A national benefits administrator has seen turnaround times rise even though staffing levels and total case volume have remained stable. Cases pass through different combinations of intake, eligibility review, specialist approval, fraud screening, and payment authorization. Shared teams serve several offices, so cases with different origins and destinations may depend on the same internal resources.

The operations group has six months of submission and completion timestamps, case categories, office identifiers, and nominal routing records for thousands of cases. A smaller audit sample includes intermediate handoff timestamps. Its current planning model predicts completion times by case category and assigned route, but actual delays exceed those estimates in recurring combinations of offices and review stages. Internal queue histories are unavailable, and employee-level activity cannot be collected under labor and privacy agreements.

Leadership needs to determine which hidden capacity or coordination problems could explain these patterns and where additional staffing or revised handoffs would most plausibly improve service. Any explanation must account for cases using overlapping routes, not just average performance by office. Rare routes have little data, urgent cases receive undocumented priority, some submission timestamps reflect delayed data entry, and actual routing sometimes differs from the recorded assignment. Different combinations of internal delays may therefore support different operational decisions while appearing equally consistent with the available records.
