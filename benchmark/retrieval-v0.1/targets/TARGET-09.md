An archive holds 113 dated versions of a novelist’s unfinished mystery, arranged in a verified chronological sequence. A critic argues that the novel’s suspense changed fundamentally when readers were told the masked visitor’s identity before the protagonist discovered it. In the earliest version, that identity remains concealed until the discovery scene; in the latest, the narrator identifies the visitor several pages earlier.

Every version contains the same clearly marked discovery scene. For this study, advance disclosure means an unambiguous statement in the narrator’s own voice that the visitor is Clara Venn. Resemblances, dialogue containing accusations, and clues from which readers might infer her identity do not count. An editorial ledger says that once this disclosure was introduced, it was retained, although the ledger has not been independently checked.

The archive provides a dated inventory and allows any version to be requested individually. Each request supplies the complete text preceding the discovery scene, but conservation restrictions permit only ten requests during the critic’s visit.

Determine the earliest surviving version that meets the disclosure criterion, narrowing its introduction sufficiently to support a claim about the novel’s changing management of reader knowledge. Any conclusion must distinguish the surviving textual evidence from assumptions about lost drafts and the ledger’s reliability.
